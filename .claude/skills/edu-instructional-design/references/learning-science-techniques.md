# Learning science techniques: when to use and when not

Background knowledge for selection. Before citing a study in a deliverable, open it (or a reliable summary) and add it to the evidence log; otherwise describe the principle without a citation.

| Technique | Use when | Avoid or adapt when | Typical implementation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Retrieval practice / active recall | Facts, terms, procedures that must be recalled later | Learner has not yet been taught the material (pre-questions are a separate, deliberate tactic) | Short questions after each chunk; free recall prompts; flashcards |
| Spaced repetition | Knowledge needed over weeks or months | One-off tasks; very short courses | Review schedule that expands intervals; resurfacing missed items |
| Chunking | Dense material; novices; small screens | Experts who already hold the larger schema | 3 to 5 new ideas per screen group; headings that name the chunk |
| Dual coding | Spatial, structural or process content (anatomy, flows, graphs) | Decorative images that add load without meaning | Labeled diagrams next to text; graph linked to numbers |
| Cognitive load management | Always, especially novices and phones | Do not remove necessary complexity; manage it | Remove extraneous detail; integrate text and image; segment; signal key points |
| Worked examples | Novices learning procedures or problem solving | Advanced learners (fade to problems) | Full example, then faded steps, then independent problem |
| Interleaving | Learners must choose between similar methods or categories | First exposure to each method | Mixed practice sets after each method is introduced |
| Elaboration and self-explanation | Conceptual understanding, transfer | Pure memorization of arbitrary facts | "Why does this work?" prompts; compare two cases |
| Adaptive learning | Mixed-ability audiences; long courses | Tiny lessons where branching costs more than it gives | Placement check; branch on errors; extra practice on weak items |
| Feedback | Every practice item | Feedback that only says right or wrong | Explain why; address the specific misconception chosen |
| Case-based learning | Professional judgment (clinical, management) | Before the learner has core concepts | Realistic case, decision points, consequences, debrief |

Widely cited syntheses to verify and cite only after opening: Dunlosky et al. (2013) review of learning techniques; cognitive load theory (Sweller and colleagues); multimedia learning principles (Mayer); expertise reversal effect (Kalyuga and colleagues); retrieval practice research (Roediger and Karpicke).
